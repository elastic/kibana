# AI-assisted contributions to Kibana

We're glad you're here. Kibana is built by a large community, and contributions of every size make it better. More and more of that work now happens with help from AI coding tools, and that's welcome too. This page explains how to use those tools in a way that keeps your contributions genuine, reviewable, and easy for the team to accept.

## Talk to us before you build

The single best thing you can do is start a conversation. Open an issue that describes what you want to change, or leave a comment on one that already exists, before you write any code. A short note about your intent gives the team a chance to point you at the right approach, flag work that's already in progress, and save you from spending an evening on something that won't land. A pull request that arrives with no prior context is hard to review and often has to be turned away, no matter how good the code is.

## Keep the change small and clear

Good contributions have sharp edges. A fix for a specific bug, a documentation page that was wrong or missing, a test that fills a real gap, an accessibility improvement that helps actual users. When a change has a clear boundary, you can run it, confirm it does what you claim, and reason about whether it's correct. Sprawling changes that touch many unrelated areas are difficult to verify and slow to merge, so aim for something you can stand behind end to end.

## Know your own diff

Whatever tools you used to get there, you are the author of the code you submit. That means reading every line, running it locally, and being ready to explain why each part is the way it is. If a reviewer asks about a decision and the honest answer is "the model wrote it that way," the change isn't ready. A simple rule works well here. If you can't explain it, don't submit it.

## Write your own words

Conversations with the team should sound like you. When you comment on an issue, respond to review feedback, or post in a Slack thread, write in your own voice about what you actually think. Replies generated by an LLM tend to be easy to spot, and they wear down the trust that makes collaboration work. Real back-and-forth, even when it's rough around the edges, is far more useful than polished text that says nothing.

## Let AI amplify you, not replace you

These tools are at their best when they help you move faster on work you already understand. Use them to draft, to explore, to handle the tedious parts, and to learn your way around unfamiliar code. Where they fall down is producing work you can't evaluate for yourself. If you couldn't have written it, you can't really vouch for it, and vouching for it is your job as the contributor. The responsibility for what you submit rests with you, never with the tool that helped you write it.

Thanks for contributing, and for doing it thoughtfully. We're looking forward to what you build.
